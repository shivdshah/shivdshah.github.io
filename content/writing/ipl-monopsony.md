I have been watching cricket for as long as I can remember. Growing up, India was the team, the World Cup was the event, and cricket was less a sport than a shared national religion. When the IPL launched in 2008 I was a child, and it immediately changed what watching cricket felt like. Suddenly the game had franchises, auctions, strategic timeouts, and a primetime slot the whole family would gather around. It felt designed for us: fast, loud, unpredictable, and over in an evening. I spent the better part of my childhood and adolescence following it obsessively.

I have remained an admirer of what the BCCI built. The ambition of the project, the speed of the execution, the way they grew something from nothing into one of the most valuable sporting properties on earth in under two decades. It is genuinely impressive, and I do not think enough people say so in a genre of writing prone to criticism. This essay comes from admiration. It also comes from studying economics, and from an inability, having done so, to stop noticing the one place where the IPL's otherwise elegant market design quietly breaks down. What follows is my attempt to work through that contradiction honestly.

</div>

The Indian Premier League is routinely described as one of the most elegant pieces of market design in the history of professional sport. Hard salary caps prevent dynasties. A public auction sets player prices with real-time transparency. Central revenue distribution keeps franchises on roughly equal financial footing. Economists spent fifty years trying to articulate what an optimally functioning sports league should look like. The IPL, largely by accident, built something close to it.

The numbers are difficult to argue with. Eight different franchises have reached the final in seventeen seasons. Six different teams have won the title. No franchise has won three consecutive seasons. The per-match media rights value of \$16.8 million places the IPL second in the world, behind only the NFL. Every franchise is profitable. Total league valuation sits at approximately \$18 billion. By almost every conventional sports economics metric, this is an extraordinary achievement. The fastest-growing major sports league on earth, built in a country with no prior franchise culture, in under two decades.

But beneath this well-designed surface lies a structural contradiction that rarely gets the examination it deserves. The IPL is both the world's most successful cricket league and one of the most effective wage suppression mechanisms in modern professional sport. The players who generate this extraordinary value receive a share of revenues that would be considered impoverished by the standards of any comparably valued league. The market design looks elegant from the outside. It was built partly, and not accidentally, on a labour market rigged from the start.

Understanding why this happened, and why no one has meaningfully challenged it, requires looking not at what the IPL got right, but at the specific and unusual nature of the power the Board of Control for Cricket in India holds over the global cricket labour market.

## The Revenue Share That Does Not Add Up

In the NFL, players receive approximately 48% of total league revenues through collective bargaining, a figure negotiated through decades of adversarial labour disputes, including a strike in 1987 and legal action in 1992. In the NBA, the split is roughly 50/50. The Basketball Related Income split sits at the centre of the collective bargaining agreement, and both sides negotiate hard over it every few years, including a lockout in 2011 that shortened the season. In Major League Baseball, despite having no salary cap at all, players receive roughly 55% of total revenues. The MLBPA is the most powerful players' union in American sport. Even in the English Premier League, where financial mismanagement is endemic, wage-to-revenue ratios regularly exceed 60%.

In the IPL, the equivalent figure is somewhere between 17% and 24%, depending on how you define the revenue base. Chennai Super Kings, one of the most commercially successful franchises and one of the few with publicly available accounts, pays its players roughly 24% of total revenues. At the league level, total player costs of approximately \$144 million per season sit against combined franchise revenues in excess of \$850 million. The gap is not marginal. It is structural, it has widened every year, and no one with institutional power has any reason to fix it.

The salary cap for the 2025 season stood at INR 120 crore (\$14.4 million) per team. That ceiling has grown at a compound annual rate of roughly 9.5% since the league's founding. Over the same period, the per-team share of central broadcast revenues has grown at closer to 11%. These numbers look similar. The difference compounds. Every season, the players' share of the economic pie gets marginally smaller. In absolute terms, the best-paid IPL players earn more than ever. In relative terms, they fall further behind the league they are generating.

## The Anatomy of a Monopsony

Economics defines a monopsonist as a single, dominant buyer in a market with many sellers. In a competitive labour market, workers offer their services across multiple potential employers, which drives wages toward the worker's marginal revenue product. In a monopsony, the single buyer sets wages below this competitive level, extracting a surplus that would otherwise accrue to workers.

The BCCI is not the only employer of cricket talent. Franchises do compete against each other for players in the auction, and that competition is genuine. When Mitchell Starc sells for ₹24.75 crore or Rishabh Pant for ₹27 crore, real price discovery is happening. But the BCCI sits above the franchise market and controls the parameters within which competition takes place. It sets the salary cap that defines how high any franchise can bid. It controls international scheduling windows that determine which players are available and when. And it controls something no amount of franchise money can easily replace: eligibility to represent India at the international level.

This last point is the mechanism making the entire system work. An Indian cricketer who defects to a competing structure faces losing their national team place. The BCCI made this threat explicit during the Indian Cricket League episode of 2007, effectively blacklisting every player who chose to participate. This was before the IPL had even launched, when the BCCI had far less commercial leverage than it does today. The message was received cleanly. No Indian player of genuine consequence has seriously challenged the BCCI's authority over their career since.

For overseas players, the leverage operates differently but arrives at the same destination. An international cricketer's participation in the IPL requires a No Objection Certificate from their national board. Their national board has its own reasons to maintain cordial relations with the BCCI, the wealthiest cricket board in the world by a distance not close. The BCCI's influence over other boards flows directly from this financial dominance. Money buys institutional compliance as reliably in cricket governance as anywhere else. A player who makes themselves difficult is a player whose board will find reasons to make the paperwork difficult.

This dual-sided control, over the franchise labour market through the cap and over the international labour market through national team selection and inter-board relations, is what distinguishes the BCCI's position from any other governing body in global sport. It is not simply that the BCCI is large. It operates without any meaningful counterweight while simultaneously holding both sides of the market.

To estimate what the IPL labour market would look like without monopsony power, we need three inputs: the revenue base, a competitive labour share benchmark from comparable leagues, and a comparison of the result with actual wages. Each step involves judgements, stated explicitly below.

### Step 1: The Revenue Base

The current IPL media rights deal (2023–2027) is worth \$6.2 billion over five years, or \$1.24 billion per year. The BCCI retains 50% of central revenues. The remaining \$620 million is distributed across franchises annually (approximately 45% equally and 5% on a performance basis). Adding franchise-specific revenues, including team sponsorships, jersey deals, ticket sales, and local partnerships, which typically contribute \$15–25 million per team, brings total franchise-level revenues to approximately \$77–87 million per team, or roughly \$850 million across the league as a whole.

### Step 2: The Competitive Labour Share Benchmark

In sports leagues where players have genuine collective bargaining power, labour's share of revenues has converged toward a narrow range. The NFL's CBA gives players 48% of total league revenues. The NBA's agreement splits Basketball Related Income approximately 50/50. Major League Baseball, with no salary cap and the most powerful union in American sport, sees players receive around 55% of revenues, though this share has been declining and is itself a source of ongoing conflict. Taking the midpoint of the range across these three leagues gives a benchmark of approximately 50%.

European football typically runs higher, at 60–70%, but this reflects an absence of salary caps rather than a negotiated outcome. It often produces clubs that lose money and is not a useful benchmark. The NFL/NBA/MLB range, representing markets where players have genuine but constrained countervailing power, is the appropriate comparison class.

| League | Labour share | Mechanism | Players' union? |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| MLB | ~55% | Free market, no cap | Yes (MLBPA) |
| NBA | ~50% | Soft cap, CBA negotiated | Yes (NBPA) |
| NFL | ~48% | Hard cap, CBA negotiated | Yes (NFLPA) |
| EPL | ~60–70% | No cap, market wages | PFA (limited) |
| **IPL** | **~17–24%** | **Hard cap, unilaterally set** | **None** |

### Step 3: The Competitive Market Wage

Applying a 50% labour share to total franchise revenues of approximately \$850 million gives a competitive player payroll of \$425 million per season across all ten franchises. This implies an average competitive salary cap of approximately \$42.5 million per team, roughly three times the current INR 120 crore (\$14.4 million) ceiling.

| Metric | Actual (2025) | Competitive benchmark |
|--------|--------------|----------------------|
| Total player costs (all teams) | ~\$144M | ~\$425M |
| Average salary cap per team | ~\$14.4M | ~\$42.5M |
| Labour share of franchise revenues | ~17–24% | ~50% |
| Highest individual contract (Rishabh Pant) | ~\$3.2M | ~\$8–12M (est.) |
| **Implied monopsony tax (per season)** | | **~\$281M annually** |

The individual player estimate deserves elaboration. Rishabh Pant is the most expensive player in IPL history at ₹27 crore (\$3.2 million for a seven-week season). He is also arguably the most bankable cricketer in India after Virat Kohli, a keeping-batting all-rounder with global commercial appeal and a compelling personal backstory. In the NBA, a player with Pant's combination of competitive value and commercial marketability would command somewhere between \$20–35 million per season. The IPL comparison is not like-for-like; the NBA season is much longer and the market is different. Even accounting for these differences, an \$8–12 million estimate for a competitive IPL contract for a player of Pant's standing is conservative.

### Step 4: How Does the BCCI Compare to Other Governing Bodies?

Not all governing bodies exploit their structural position equivalently. The comparison below isolates what makes the BCCI's position unusual, rather than simply powerful.

| Governing body | Sets wage ceiling? | Controls nat'l team? | Players can strike? | Labour share |
|----------------|-------------------|---------------------|--------------------:|-------------|
| BCCI (IPL) | Yes, unilateral | Yes | No union | 17–24% |
| NFL (NFLPA) | Yes, via CBA | No national team | Yes, has struck | ~48% |
| NBA (NBPA) | Yes, via CBA | No national team | Yes, locked out | ~50% |
| FIFA | No | Indirectly | Yes (Bosman) | 60–70% (EPL) |
| ICC | No | Shared with boards | No equivalent | N/A |

The key distinction is that the BCCI sets the wage ceiling unilaterally, without collective bargaining, while simultaneously controlling the career risk associated with challenging that ceiling through national team selection. No other governing body in major professional sport combines both of these levers. The NFL and NBA both set wage ceilings, but those ceilings are negotiated outcomes, enforced by contracts between equals rather than imposed by an organisation deciding whether you get to play for your country. FIFA does not set club wages at all. The Bosman ruling of 1995 effectively took that power away from European football's governing bodies permanently, shifting leverage decisively toward players.

The BCCI is in a category of its own. It holds more unchecked wage-setting power than any governing body in a sport of comparable commercial scale.

## Why Players Accept It

The obvious question is why players have not pushed back. The answer has several layers.

For Indian players, the mechanism is clear. International cricket is not a career option among others. For a player who grows up in India, representing the national side is the highest possible aspiration, cultural, financial, and personal. The BCCI's control over that aspiration is total. A wage dispute costing a few crore is recoverable. Being dropped from the national setup for being difficult to manage is not. The implicit threat is credible, it has been exercised historically, and every Indian cricketer old enough to remember 2007 understands what happens when the BCCI decides to make an example.

For overseas players, the calculation is different but the conclusion is similar. Even at its current suppressed wage levels, the IPL offers the highest franchise cricket contracts available to non-Indian players. A South African or English cricketer earning ₹10 crore for seven weeks earns more than they would for a full international season with their national board. The opportunity cost of missing the IPL is large enough to make wage suppression a tolerated condition of entry.

A subtler mechanism also deserves attention. The auction creates the visible appearance of a competitive market. Players watch their names appear on a screen and see franchises bidding against each other in real time. When Rishabh Pant sells for ₹27 crore, the highest price in IPL history, the number feels enormous because the comparison class is other IPL contracts. The fact that in the NBA, a player of Pant's commercial and competitive value would command something closer to ₹200 crore for a longer season is not visible in the auction room. The auction is genuinely competitive within its constraints. The constraints themselves are the point, and they are invisible in the moment that matters.

This is how well-designed wage suppression works. It provides enough apparent market activity to make the structural ceiling above it seem like a natural feature of the game rather than a choice made by the people who benefit from it most.

## What Changes This, and When

The IPL will face a labour market correction. The question is what form it takes and over what timeline.

The American precedent is instructive because it has played out several times across different sports. The NFL's 48% revenue share did not emerge because the NFL's owners developed a sudden interest in fairness. It emerged because the NFLPA developed the institutional capacity to force the issue, including through antitrust litigation threatening the league's legal structure. The NBA's 50/50 split came after a lockout costing both sides hundreds of millions of dollars, establishing conclusively that players were willing to withhold their labour. The pattern is consistent: governing bodies holding unchecked wage-setting power do not give it up voluntarily. They give it up to organised players, not to disorganised ones.

Cricket does not yet have this infrastructure. There is no IPL Players' Association with anything resembling bargaining rights. There is no collective bargaining mechanism. There is no Bosman ruling waiting to happen, because there is no legal framework that would straightforwardly generate one. The BCCI is a private body. Its relationships with players are mediated through BCCI contracts and national board contracts. Indian labour law does not offer the same mechanisms European competition law eventually provided for football players. The institutional prerequisites for a labour market correction simply do not exist today.

What does exist is the slow build of competing power centres outside India. As the SA20 and ILT20 mature, as Major League Cricket establishes itself in the United States, as the global franchise calendar becomes denser and more remunerative, the BCCI's ability to threaten overseas players with exclusion from their league ecosystem diminishes. The monopsony is most absolute for Indian players, where the threat of international exclusion remains credible and has no parallel. It is weakest for overseas players, where it has always depended on the BCCI's ability to maintain informal influence over other boards, influence eroding as those boards develop their own commercial interests to protect.

The endgame is not a dramatic rupture. The more likely path is a gradual repricing: the salary cap grows faster than 9.5% per annum as competing leagues bid up the marginal price of elite overseas talent, which in turn creates pressure on the cap for Indian players, which in turn forces a renegotiation of the implicit social contract between the BCCI and the country's cricketers. The monopsony weakens slowly at the edges before it weakens at the centre.

## The Conclusion the Celebration Misses

The IPL is routinely discussed as a cricket story. It should be more precisely understood as a market design story, specifically, a story about what happens when market design is optimised for some participants and not others.

The franchise market works beautifully. Competitive balance is real. Price discovery in the auction is genuine. Revenue distribution across teams is equitable. These are genuine achievements, and they deserve the attention they get. But they are achievements accruing to franchises and to the BCCI. The players who make these achievements possible, who provide the competitive uncertainty, the athleticism, the moments shifting 600 million viewers to the edge of their seats, are the one constituency for whom the market was not designed to work well, and the one constituency with the least institutional power to change that.

The IPL is often described as an accidentally optimal sports league. That is a generous framing. What it built was an optimally designed league for everyone except the people playing the game. The market producing results so admirable at the franchise level produces, at the labour market level, an implied annual transfer of roughly \$281 million from the athletes who generate the value to the institutions that capture it.

Seventeen seasons in, that transfer has not attracted the scrutiny it deserves. This is partly because the amounts, in IPL terms, look large. Rishabh Pant earning ₹27 crore for seven weeks is genuinely extraordinary in the context of Indian salaries, and in the context of what cricketers have historically earned. The relevant comparison is not what cricketers used to earn. It is what players of equivalent commercial value earn in other sports businesses of equivalent scale. On that comparison, the numbers are not flattering.

The most important test the IPL will face over the next decade is not whether it can maintain competitive balance between franchises. It has already solved that problem well. The test is whether the governing body that built the best-designed sports league in the world is capable of designing a labour market equally fair to the people inside it. History suggests this does not happen without a fight.
